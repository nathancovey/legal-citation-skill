# Quotations

## Length decides the format

- **49 words or fewer:** keep it in the text, inside quotation marks.
- **50 words or more:** use a block quotation — indented on both sides, single-spaced, with **no** quotation marks. In a memo or brief, the citation starts at the left margin on the line after the block.

## Punctuation

- Commas and periods go inside the closing quotation mark. Other punctuation goes outside unless it belongs to the quoted text.
- A quotation inside a quotation takes single marks:

  > *Andrews*, 843 A.2d at 139 (concluding "a reasonable officer should not have known that her words and actions were 'reasonably likely to evoke an incriminating response'").

## Omissions

An ellipsis is three periods separated by spaces, with a space before and after: ` . . . `

**Never begin a quotation with an ellipsis.**

### Quoting a phrase or clause that is woven into your sentence

Don't mark anything left out before or after it. Use an ellipsis only for words left out in the middle.

> The court described "an inappropriately expansive response" to a simple question.

### Quoting a full sentence

Suppose the source reads: *The court infers from Defendant's testimony at the suppression hearing that when he is nervous, Defendant becomes overly talkative.*

| What you leave out | How to mark it |
|---|---|
| The beginning | Capitalize the first letter in brackets if it isn't already a capital: `"[W]hen he is nervous, Defendant becomes overly talkative."` If the first word is already capitalized (for example "I"), mark nothing. |
| The middle | An ellipsis where words are missing: `"A relatively simple . . . question could elicit an inappropriately expansive response."` |
| The end | An ellipsis, then the sentence's final punctuation: `"Unforeseeable statements volunteered to the police are not the product . . . ."` |
| Anything after the last sentence you quote | Nothing. |
| Text after one sentence, before you resume quoting | Keep the first sentence's final punctuation, then an ellipsis, then the rest. |

Don't use an ellipsis when you have changed a word rather than removed one. That is an alteration.

## Alterations

- Changing a letter's case: brackets — `"[T]he court …"`
- Substituting or inserting words: brackets — `"[the officer] asked …"`
- Letters left out of a word: empty brackets — `judgment[]`
- An error in the original: `[sic]` right after it.
- After the citation, disclose changes in a parenthetical: `(emphasis added)`, `(alteration in original)`, `(citations omitted)`, `(footnote omitted)`. See the parenthetical order in `signals-and-parentheticals.md`.

**Brackets may change form, never meaning.** Turning a court's "could" into "[w]ould" upgrades a possibility into a likelihood — that is a misquote, not an alteration. Silently dropping a word inside quotation marks ("the back seat of a patrol car" quoted as "the back of a patrol car") is also a misquote.

## Candor

The omission rules say when you *may* leave words out unmarked. They don't make every omission fair. In objective writing — a memo predicting an outcome — an omission that changes the meaning still misleads.

Example: a witness was asked whether he had dropped anyone off at a building, and answered, "Yes, okay, I dropped a woman off there earlier tonight. Things got a little out of control." Quoting only the sentences after "Yes, okay," while arguing the statements weren't responsive to the question hides the words that were. Quote the whole answer and make the argument openly.

When you review someone else's quotation, flag omissions that change the meaning, even if they're technically permitted.
