# Quotations

## Length decides the format (B5, Rule 5.1)

- **49 words or fewer:** keep it in the text, inside quotation marks. Put the citation right after the closing quotation mark.
- **50 words or more:** use a block quotation. Indent it on both sides, single-space it, justify it, and use **no** quotation marks around it. Quotation marks *inside* the block appear as they do in the original. In a memo or brief, the citation starts at the left margin on the line after the block, not indented.
- **Paragraphs in a block quotation** (Rule 5.1(a)(iii)): indent the first line of each quoted paragraph. Indent the first line of the block only if your quotation starts at the start of a paragraph in the source. An ellipsis indented on its own line marks an omission at the start of a later paragraph. Four periods (`. . . .`) indented on their own line mark one or more whole paragraphs left out.

## Punctuation

- Commas and periods go inside the closing quotation mark. Other punctuation goes outside unless it belongs to the quoted text.
- A quotation inside a quotation takes single marks. A third level goes back to double:

  > *Andrews*, 843 A.2d at 139 (concluding "a reasonable officer should not have known that her words and actions were 'reasonably likely to evoke an incriminating response'").

- **Ellipses and brackets are always roman**, even inside an italicized or underlined quotation (Rule 2.1(g), Rule 2.2(d)).

## Omissions (Rule 5.3)

An ellipsis is three periods separated by spaces, with a space before and after: ` . . . `

**Never begin a quotation with an ellipsis.** Don't use one when you have changed a word rather than removed one. That is an alteration.

### Quoting a phrase or clause that is woven into your sentence

Don't mark anything left out before or after it. Use an ellipsis only for words left out in the middle.

> ✓ The court described "an inappropriately expansive response" to a simple question.
> ✗ The court described ". . . an inappropriately expansive response . . ." to a simple question.

### Quoting a full sentence

Suppose the source reads: *The court infers from Defendant's testimony at the suppression hearing that when he is nervous, Defendant becomes overly talkative.*

| What you leave out | How to mark it |
|---|---|
| The beginning | Capitalize the first letter in brackets if it isn't already a capital: `"[W]hen he is nervous, Defendant becomes overly talkative."` If the first word is already capitalized (for example "I"), mark nothing. |
| The middle | An ellipsis where words are missing: `"A relatively simple . . . question could elicit an inappropriately expansive response."` |
| The end | An ellipsis, then the sentence's final punctuation: `"Unforeseeable statements volunteered to the police are not the product . . . ."` |
| Anything after the last sentence you quote | Nothing. |
| Text after one sentence, before you resume quoting | Keep the first sentence's final punctuation, then an ellipsis, then the rest: `"National borders are less of a barrier to economic exchange now than at almost any other time in history. . . . [E]conomic activity continues its relentless drive toward world-wide scope, [so] trademarks become even more important."` (Rule 5.3(b)(v)) |
| The end of one sentence **and** what follows it, before you resume | One ellipsis covers both, then the sentence's punctuation: `"National borders are less of a barrier . . . . [E]conomic activity continues its relentless drive toward world-wide scope, [so] trademarks become even more important."` (Rule 5.3(b)(vi)) |

A question mark or exclamation point that ends the shortened sentence follows the ellipsis: `"Why, then, are certain scholars advocating less . . . ?"`

### Omitted citations and footnotes (Rule 5.3(c), Rule 5.2(d))

- Mark an omitted citation or footnote call with a parenthetical after your citation: `(citation omitted)`, `(citations omitted)`, `(footnote omitted)`, `(footnotes omitted)`. **Never** with an ellipsis.
- Don't mark the omission of a citation or footnote call that came after the last word you quote.

## Alterations (Rule 5.2)

- Changing a letter's case: brackets: `"[T]he court …"`
- Substituting or inserting words: brackets: `"[the officer] asked …"`
- Letters left out of a word: empty brackets: `judgment[]`
- A significant error in the original: `[sic]` right after it, the error left as is.
- After the citation, disclose changes in a parenthetical: `(emphasis added)`, `(alteration in original)`, `(citations omitted)`, `(footnote omitted)`. See the parenthetical order in `signals-and-parentheticals.md`.
- **Never write `(emphasis in original)`.** Emphasis in the original is reproduced silently (Rule 5.2(d)(iii)).

**Brackets may change form, never meaning.** Turning a court's "could" into "[w]ould" upgrades a possibility into a likelihood. That is a misquote, not an alteration. Silently dropping a word inside quotation marks ("the back seat of a patrol car" quoted as "the back of a patrol car") is also a misquote.

## Quotations within quotations (Rule 5.2(e)–(f), B5.3)

This changed in the 22d edition. **`(internal quotation marks omitted)` and `(cleaned up)` are not 22d-edition devices.** Don't write them, and flag them in review. There are three correct ways to handle a court's quotation of another source.

### 1. Keep the internal marks and attribute them

The default. Keep the inner quotation marks as single marks, and add a `(quoting …)` parenthetical after any other parenthetical (Rule 5.2(e)):

> ✓ Chief Judge Wright noted Congress's "*firm resolve* to insure that the CIA's 'power that flows from money and stealth' could not be turned loose in domestic investigations of Americans." *Marks v. CIA*, 590 F.2d 997, 1008 (D.C. Cir. 1978) (Wright, C.J., concurring in part and dissenting in part) (emphasis added) (quoting *Weissman v. CIA*, 565 F.2d 692, 695 (D.C. Cir. 1977)).

### 2. Drop internal marks that span the whole quotation (Rule 5.2(f))

Omit the inner marks **only** when the opening mark is at the very beginning of your in-line quotation and the closing mark is at the very end. **Keep the `(quoting …)` parenthetical** anyway (Rule 5.2(f)(iii)).

Original: *If "the agency's answer is based on a permissible construction of the statute," that is the end of the matter.*

> ✓ The dispositive question is whether "the agency's answer is based on a permissible construction of the statute." Then cite the opinion you read, ending with a `(quoting …)` parenthetical for the source it quoted.
> ✗ The dispositive question is whether "'the agency's answer is based on a permissible construction of the statute.'"

But:

- **If the inner quotation covers only part of what you quote, keep its marks.** Original: *In this context, "the distinction between 'jurisdictional' and 'non-jurisdictional' interpretations is a mirage."*

  > ✓ The Court noted that, "[i]n this context, 'the distinction between "jurisdictional" and "non-jurisdictional" interpretations is a mirage.'"
  > ✗ The Court noted that, "[i]n this context, 'the distinction between jurisdictional and non-jurisdictional interpretations is a mirage.'"

- **Never strip more than one level of nested marks** under this rule (Rule 5.2(f)(ii)).
- **Never omit internal marks in a block quotation** (Rule 5.2(f)(iv)).

### 3. Modify the quotation and say "(citation modified)" (B5.3)

New in the 22d edition. When a quotation includes material quoted from another source, you may, for clarity:

- strip internal quotation marks, brackets, ellipses, internal citations, and footnote reference numbers,
- leave the original sources of the inner quotations out of your citation, and
- change capitalization without brackets.

Then add `(citation modified)` after the citation. It replaces the `(alteration in original)`, `(quoting …)`, and similar parentheticals for those changes.

> Before: "[A]n 'on the merits' adjudication is" understood as "one that actually 'pass[es] directly on the substance of [a particular] claim' before the court." *Semtek Int'l Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.*, 531 U.S. 497, 501–02 (2001) (alteration in original) (quoting Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 19 cmt. a (A.L.I. 1982)).
>
> ✓ After: "An on the merits adjudication is" understood as "one that actually passes directly on the substance of a particular claim before the court." *Semtek Int'l Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.*, 531 U.S. 497, 501–02 (2001) (citation modified).

Limits:

- **Only those changes.** Everything else must match the source word for word. Any other change, such as an omission of your own or added emphasis, is still marked under Rules 5.2 and 5.3: an ellipsis, brackets, `(emphasis added)`.
- **It is a representation.** Writing `(citation modified)` affirms that the changes were made only for readability and that the quotation otherwise faithfully reproduces the source (B5.3). Don't use it to smooth over a change of meaning.
- **Use it instead of `(cleaned up)`**, `(internal quotation marks omitted)`, or any similar parenthetical (B5.3 tip).
- B5.3 is a Bluepages rule, for court documents and memos. The Whitepages have no counterpart, so in a law review piece use methods 1 and 2 unless the journal's style sheet says otherwise.

### Reviewing someone else's draft

| As written | Fix |
|---|---|
| `… (2001) (internal quotation marks omitted).` | Restore the internal marks and add `(quoting …)`. Or, if all the changes are ones B5.3 allows, replace the parenthetical with `(citation modified)`. |
| `… (2001) (cleaned up).` | `(citation modified)`, after checking that every change is one B5.3 allows. |
| Inner marks dropped where they covered only part of the quotation | Restore them. |
| Inner marks dropped, and no `(quoting …)` or `(citation modified)` | Add the parenthetical. |

## Candor

The omission rules say when you *may* leave words out unmarked. They don't make every omission fair. In objective writing, such as a memo predicting an outcome, an omission that changes the meaning still misleads.

Example: a witness was asked whether he had dropped anyone off at a building, and answered, "Yes, okay, I dropped a woman off there earlier tonight. Things got a little out of control." Quoting only the sentences after "Yes, okay," while arguing the statements weren't responsive to the question hides the words that were. Quote the whole answer and make the argument openly.

When you review someone else's quotation, flag omissions that change the meaning, even if they're technically permitted.
