# Typeface and placement

## Practitioner style (briefs, motions, memos)

Italicize **or** underline the following. Pick one and use it throughout.

- Case names, full and short, including `v.` and procedural phrases (`In re`, `ex rel.`)
- Signals: *See*, *See also*, *Cf.*, *But see*, *See, e.g.,* (everything except the final comma)
- Explanatory phrases introducing case history: *aff'd*, *rev'd*, *cert. denied*
- *Id.* and *supra*
- Phrases introducing related authority: *quoted in*, *cited in*
- Titles of books and articles
- In your prose: publication titles, words italicized in the original of a quotation, and uncommon foreign words

Leave these in roman type:

- volume, reporter, pages, the court-and-year parenthetical
- **the comma after a case name** — `State v. Bonner, 643 A.2d …` (italics stop before the comma)

Punctuation that falls *inside* the italicized material is italicized with it. That is why **the period in *Id.* is italicized**: it's part of the abbreviation.

## Academic style (law review footnotes)

- In a full citation in a footnote, the case name is **roman**.
- Short-form case names, signals, *Id.*, and procedural and explanatory phrases are **italic**.
- A case name used in a textual sentence — in the main text or in a footnote's prose — is **italic**.
- Books and periodicals use large-and-small capitals for authors and titles. Follow the publication's own style sheet.

## Checking typeface in a word processor

Italics that look right on screen are often slightly wrong in the file:

- the emphasis stops one character early, or covers a trailing space,
- the emphasis runs onto the comma after the case name,
- the period in *Id.* is left roman.

Check those three spots specifically.

## Citation sentences and citation clauses

| Form | Supports | Mechanics |
|---|---|---|
| **Citation sentence** | The whole preceding sentence | Follows that sentence; starts with a capital letter, ends with a period. |
| **Citation clause** | Only part of a sentence | Set off by commas right after the part it supports; lowercase unless the first word is otherwise capitalized; no period unless it ends the sentence. |

Both are correct. Prefer citation sentences when you can, because clauses break up the prose.

**A split citation is also correct.** You can name the case in your sentence and give the rest of the citation in the citation sentence that follows. Because the name is in the text, the citation may leave it out:

> In *State v. Bonner*, the court held that being placed in a patrol car does not by itself make an encounter prolonged. 643 A.2d 13, 15 (Del. Super. Ct. 1995).

**Never report placement as an error.** A reviewer that "fixes" a valid split citation by adding the name back, or moves a citation sentence into a clause, creates churn. It can also contradict itself across two comments on the same citation.

In court documents and memos, citations go in the text, not in footnotes, unless local rules say otherwise.
