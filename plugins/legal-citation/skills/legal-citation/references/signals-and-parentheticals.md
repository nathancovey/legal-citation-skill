# Signals and explanatory parentheticals

## Signals

A signal tells the reader how the cited authority relates to your sentence.

| Signal | Use when the authority… | Parenthetical |
|---|---|---|
| *(none)* | states the proposition directly, is the source of a quotation, or is named in your sentence | If relevance is unclear |
| *E.g.,* | states it, and other authorities do too, but citing them all wouldn't help | If relevance is unclear |
| *Accord* | states or clearly supports it, alongside an authority you quoted or named | If relevance is unclear |
| *See* | supports it, but only through an inferential step — the proposition follows from the authority without being stated in it | If relevance is unclear |
| *See also* | adds support **after** authority that directly supports the proposition has already been cited or discussed | **Always** |
| *Cf.* | supports a different but analogous proposition | **Always** |
| *Compare … with …* | is worth comparing with another authority to illustrate the point | **Always**, for each authority |
| *Contra* | directly states the opposite | If relevance is unclear |
| *But see* | clearly supports the opposite | If relevance is unclear |
| *But cf.* | supports a proposition analogous to the opposite | If relevance is unclear |
| *See generally* | provides background | If relevance is unclear |

"If relevance is unclear" is a real requirement, not a suggestion: whenever a reader might not see why an authority supports your point, the parenthetical is required.

Mechanics:

- Italicize (or underline) signals. In `See, e.g.,` everything is italicized except the final comma.
- Capitalize a signal that begins a citation sentence; lowercase it inside a citation clause.
- When one citation sentence uses several signals, order them as in the table. Separate authorities with semicolons. Within a signal you may put the most helpful or most authoritative source first.
- **Don't repeat a signal** for consecutive authorities that share it: `See A; B.`, not `See A; see B.`

### Common mistakes

- **"See also" with nothing to be "also" to.** If this is the first or only authority for the point, *see also* is wrong.
- **"See" for a direct statement.** If your sentence restates what the court held, use no signal.

### The signal can't fix the sentence

A citation sentence vouches for the **entire** preceding sentence. If that sentence states a fact from your own record, no case can support it, and changing the signal doesn't help.

> ✗ The officers told her she could leave once the paperwork was done. *See Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15 (…).

*Bonner* knows nothing about what these officers said. Split the record fact from the law:

> ✓ The officers told her she could leave once the paperwork was done. [Record cite.] *Bonner* found no custody on materially the same assurance: the defendant "would have been free to leave as soon as the traffic stop paperwork had been processed." 643 A.2d at 15.

## Explanatory parentheticals

A parenthetical tells the reader why the citation matters. Make it carry the legal point, not just a fact.

- Begin with a **present participle** in lowercase: `holding`, `finding`, `concluding`, `explaining`, `noting`, `reasoning`. For what a court decided, use `holding`; vaguer verbs like `showing` blur whether it was a holding.
- Articles such as "the" may be dropped if the meaning stays clear.
- If the parenthetical quotes one or more **full sentences**, start with the capital letter, include the closing punctuation, and use no participle: `(“The Court finds that the totality of the circumstances surrounding the traffic stop and subsequent questioning of the defendant were not prolonged or coercive.”)`. Don't write `(explaining that “The …”)`. Make sure the sentence really ends where you end it — a clause followed by a semicolon in the opinion is not a full sentence.
- If it quotes only a **phrase**, use a participle and work the phrase in: `(finding the encounter not “prolonged or coercive”)`.

Weak and strong versions of the same parenthetical:

> ✗ *See Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15 (showing suspect was free to go as soon as the paperwork was complete).
>
> ✓ *Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15 (holding a defendant placed in a patrol car was not in custody because he would have been free to leave once the traffic-stop paperwork was processed).

## Order of parentheticals

When a citation needs several, use this order:

1. date or court-and-year
2. `[hereinafter …]`
3. `(en banc)`
4. `(Smith, J., concurring)` / `(dissenting)`
5. `(plurality opinion)`
6. `(per curiam)`
7. `(alteration in original)`
8. `(emphasis added)`
9. `(footnote omitted)`
10. `(citations omitted)`
11. `(quoting …)`
12. `(citing …)`
13. a URL and `(last visited …)`
14. the explanatory parenthetical
15. prior or subsequent history

## Citing language a court quoted from another case

Cite the opinion you actually read, then add `(quoting …)` or `(citing …)`:

```
State v. Bonner, 643 A.2d 13, 14 (Del. Super. Ct. 1995) (citing Berkemer v. McCarty, 468 U.S. 420, 425 (1984)).
```

If you can see the language only through the intermediate case and can't see the original's page, cite the intermediate case alone. Never supply the original's pincite from memory.
