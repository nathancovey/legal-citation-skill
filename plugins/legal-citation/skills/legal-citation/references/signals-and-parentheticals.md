# Signals and explanatory parentheticals

## Signals

A signal tells the reader how the cited authority relates to your sentence (B1.2, Rule 1.2). Use them in this order, which is also their order in a string citation:

| Signal | Use when the authority… | Explanatory parenthetical |
|---|---|---|
| *(none)* | directly states the proposition, is the source of a quotation, or is named in your sentence | If relevance is unclear |
| *E.g.,* | states it, and other authorities do too, but citing them all wouldn't help | If relevance is unclear |
| *Accord* | states or clearly supports it, alongside an authority you quoted or named | If relevance is unclear |
| *See* | supports it, but only through an inferential step: the proposition follows from the authority without being stated in it | If relevance is unclear |
| *See also* | adds support **after** authority that directly supports the proposition has already been cited or discussed | **Always** (Rule 1.2(a)) |
| *Cf.* | supports a different but analogous proposition | **Always** (Rule 1.2(a)) |
| *Compare … with …* | is similar in important respects to another authority, which illustrates the point | **Always**, after each authority (Rule 1.2(b)) |
| *Contrast … with …* | differs in important respects from another authority, which illustrates the point | **Always**, after each authority (Rule 1.2(b)) |
| *Contra* | directly states the opposite | If relevance is unclear |
| *But see* | clearly supports the opposite | If relevance is unclear |
| *But cf.* | supports a proposition analogous to the opposite | **Always** (Rule 1.2(c)) |
| *See generally* | provides helpful background | **Always**, after each authority (Rule 1.2(d)) |

"If relevance is unclear" is a requirement, not a suggestion. Whenever a reader might not see why an authority supports your point, the parenthetical is required (Rule 1.5(a)).

> ✗ *See generally* Akhil Reed Amar, *Reports of My Death Are Greatly Exaggerated: A Reply*, 138 U. Pa. L. Rev. 1651 (1990).
> ✓ *See generally* Akhil Reed Amar, *Reports of My Death Are Greatly Exaggerated: A Reply*, 138 U. Pa. L. Rev. 1651 (1990) (arguing that the author and the two-tier theory of federal jurisdiction are still viable).
> ✗ *But cf. Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15.
> ✓ *But cf. Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15 (finding no custody where the defendant sat in a patrol car while paperwork was processed).

### Mechanics

- **Typeface.** Italicize (or underline) signals. In *See, e.g.,* and *But see, e.g.,* everything is italic except the final comma (B1.2 tip, Rule 1.2(a)). A signal used as the verb of an ordinary sentence is roman: "For a fuller account, see *Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15." (Rule 1.2(e), Rule 2.1(d)).
- **Capitalization.** Capitalize a signal that begins a citation sentence. Lowercase it inside a citation clause (B1.2 tip).
- ***Compare … with …*** and ***Contrast … with …*** always come in pairs. Put a comma before *with*, and before *and* if you add authorities: *Compare* A (…), *and* B (…), *with* C (…).
- **Drop "but"** from *but see* and *but cf.* when the signal follows another negative signal in the same string: *Contra* A; *see* B. (Rule 1.2(c)).
- **Pincites are required** for every citation except one introduced by *see generally* (Rule 1.2(f)).

### Strings of signals (Rule 1.3, Rule 1.4)

- Order signals as in the table. *E.g.* takes the position of the signal it's attached to.
- **Signals of one type go in one citation sentence, separated by semicolons.** The types are supportive (none through *cf.*), comparative (*compare*, *contrast*), contradictory (*contra* through *but cf.*), and background (*see generally*). **Each type starts a new citation sentence.** Inside a citation clause, one string may mix types (Rule 1.3).

  > ✓ *See* A; *cf.* B (…). *But see* C. *See generally* D (…).
  > ✗ *See* A; *but see* C.

- **Don't repeat a signal** for consecutive authorities that share it: *See* A; B. Not *See* A; *see* B.
- **Within a signal,** separate authorities with semicolons and order them logically. Put an authority first when it is considerably more helpful or authoritative than the others (B1.2, Rule 1.4). The 22d edition no longer prescribes the old ranking by type of authority and court. Order short forms as if they were full citations.

### Common mistakes

- **"See also" with nothing to be "also" to.** If this is the first or only authority for the point, *see also* is wrong.
- **"See" for a direct statement.** If your sentence restates what the court held, use no signal.
- **A *see also*, *cf.*, *but cf.*, *compare*, *contrast*, or *see generally* without a parenthetical.**

### The signal can't fix the sentence

A citation sentence vouches for the **entire** preceding sentence. If that sentence states a fact from your own record, no case can support it, and changing the signal doesn't help.

> ✗ The officers told her she could leave once the paperwork was done. *See Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15 (…).

*Bonner* knows nothing about what these officers said. Split the record fact from the law:

> ✓ The officers told her she could leave once the paperwork was done. R. at 12. *Bonner* found no custody on materially the same assurance: the defendant "would have been free to leave as soon as the traffic stop paperwork had been processed." 643 A.2d at 15.

Record citations follow `court-documents.md`.

## Explanatory parentheticals (B1.3, Rule 1.5(a))

A parenthetical tells the reader why the citation matters. Make it carry the legal point, not just a fact. It comes after the court-and-year parenthetical and before any subsequent history.

It takes one of three forms:

1. **A present participial phrase** in lowercase: `holding`, `finding`, `concluding`, `explaining`, `noting`, `reasoning`. For what a court decided, use `holding`. Vaguer verbs like `showing` blur whether it was a holding.
2. **A quoted sentence**, when the parenthetical quotes one or more full sentences, or material that reads as a full sentence. Start with a capital, include the closing punctuation, and use no participle:
   `(“The Court finds that the totality of the circumstances surrounding the traffic stop and subsequent questioning of the defendant were not prolonged or coercive.”)`
3. **A short statement**, when a full participial phrase is unnecessary in context: `(health-related water quality)`, `(conservation of protected areas)` (Rule 1.5(a)(i)).

Rules for each:

- Never begin a non-quoting parenthetical with a capital or end it with a period.
- Articles such as "the" may be dropped if the meaning stays clear.
- A quoted sentence keeps its quotation marks and its capital. Don't write `(explaining that “The …”)`:

  > ✗ `(explaining that “[w]e are a legalized culture. If law is where racism is, then law is where we must confront it . . . .”)`
  > ✓ `(“We are a legalized culture. If law is where racism is, then law is where we must confront it . . . .”)`

- If you present a clause as a full sentence and the court's sentence went on (past a semicolon, say), the end is an omission. Mark it with an ellipsis before the period, under Rule 5.3(b)(iii): `(“National borders are less of a barrier to economic exchange now than at almost any other time . . . .”)`. Check the source: a clause that ends at a semicolon only *reads* as a sentence once you mark the cut.
- If it quotes only a **phrase**, use a participle and work the phrase in: `(finding the encounter not “prolonged or coercive”)`.

Weak and strong versions of the same parenthetical:

> ✗ *See Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15 (showing suspect was free to go as soon as the paperwork was complete).
>
> ✓ *Bonner*, 643 A.2d at 15 (holding a defendant placed in a patrol car was not in custody because he would have been free to leave once the traffic-stop paperwork was processed).

## Weight-of-authority parentheticals (B10.1.5, Rule 10.6.1)

Add a parenthetical right after the date when the cited language is not the single, clear holding of a majority. It is required, not optional:

| Situation | Parenthetical |
|---|---|
| A concurrence or dissent | `(Breyer, J., dissenting)`, `(Thomas, J., concurring in the judgment)`, `(Wright, C.J., concurring in part and dissenting in part)` |
| A plurality | `(plurality opinion)` |
| Dictum, an alternative holding, or an implication | `(dictum)`, `(alternative holding)`, `(by implication)` |
| The court sat en banc | `(en banc)` |
| An opinion "by the court" so labeled | `(per curiam)` |
| A disposition without opinion | `(mem.)`. Not for opinions merely titled "memorandum decision." |
| A table decision | `(unpublished table decision)` |
| A split you want to show | `(5-4 decision)` |

Judges' names follow B9 and Rule 9: `Holmes, J.`, `Roberts, C.J.`, `Black & White, JJ.` Pincite a separate opinion to the page you rely on, after the first page of the *case*, not the first page of the separate opinion: `Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186, 297 (1962) (Frankfurter, J., dissenting).` (Rule 3.2(a)).

Don't put in a parenthetical what an explanatory phrase can say. `aff'd per curiam` is subsequent history, not `(per curiam)` on the lower-court cite (Rule 10.6.1(a)).

## Order of parentheticals (Rule 1.5(b), Rule 10.6.4)

When a citation needs several, use this order (Rule 1.5(b)):

1. date or court-and-year
2. `[hereinafter …]`
3. `(en banc)`
4. `(Smith, J., concurring)` / `(dissenting)`
5. `(plurality opinion)`
6. `(per curiam)`
7. `(alteration in original)`
8. `(emphasis added)`
9. `(footnote omitted)`
10. `(citations omitted)`
11. `(quoting …)`
12. `(citing …)`
13. a URL and `(last visited …)`
14. the explanatory parenthetical
15. prior or subsequent history

Rule 10.6.4 states the same order for cases in three tiers: weight of authority (3–6), then quoting or citing (11–12), then explanatory (14).

> ✓ `Wolf v. Colorado, 338 U.S. 25, 47 (1949) (Rutledge, J., dissenting) (rejecting the Court's conception of the exclusionary rule), aff'g 187 P.2d 926 (Colo. 1947).`

**`(citation modified)`** (B5.3) isn't in the Rule 1.5(b) list. B5.3's own example puts it right after the court-and-year parenthetical. It takes the place of the alteration, omission, and quoting parentheticals it replaces (items 7–12), so it goes before any explanatory parenthetical. See `quotations.md`.

### Nesting

When the parenthetical's own quotation needs a quoting or citing parenthetical, nest it inside, after the closing quotation mark (Rule 1.5(b), Rule 10.6.4):

> ✓ `Kansas v. Crane, 534 U.S. 407, 409 (2002) (“[T]he statutory criterion for confinement embodied in the statute’s words ‘mental abnormality or personality disorder’ satisfied ‘“substantive” due process requirements.’” (quoting Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346, 356 (1997))).`
> ✓ `Fullilove v. Klutznick, 448 U.S. 448, 519 (1980) (Marshall, J., concurring) (noting that conventional strict scrutiny is “strict in theory, but fatal in fact” (citing Regents of the Univ. of Cal. v. Bakke, 438 U.S. 265, 362 (1978))).`

The same applies to `(emphasis added)` for emphasis inside a parenthetical quotation: `Baze v. Rees, 553 U.S. 35, 48 (2008) (plurality opinion) (“This Court has never invalidated a State's chosen procedure for carrying out a sentence of death as the infliction of cruel and unusual punishment.” (emphasis added)).` (with "never" italicized).

## Citing language a court quoted from another case (Rule 10.6.3, Rule 5.2(e))

Cite the opinion you actually read, then add `(quoting …)` or `(citing …)`:

```
State v. Bonner, 643 A.2d 13, 14 (Del. Super. Ct. 1995) (citing Berkemer v. McCarty, 468 U.S. 420, 425 (1984)).
```

- **Whenever possible, attribute a quotation within a quotation to its original source** with a `(quoting …)` parenthetical (Rule 5.2(e)), or modify the quotation under B5.3 (see `quotations.md`).
- **The cited case inside the parenthetical follows the usual rules**: its own typeface (italic case name in a brief or memo), its own pincite, and a short form if it has already been cited in full (Rule 10.6.3). The words "quoting" and "citing" themselves are roman (Rule 2.1(e)).
- **One level is enough.** If the case you quote was itself quoting a third case, you need only the first `(quoting …)`. Add the next level only if it matters (Rule 10.6.3).
- **Several sources quoted in one passage** go in one parenthetical, in order: `(first quoting A; then quoting B; and then quoting C)` (Rule 1.5(b)).
- **`(quoting …)` versus `quoted in`.** Use the parenthetical for what *your* source quotes or cites. Use the italic phrase *quoted in* or *cited in*, with no parentheses, for a later work that quotes *your* source (Rule 1.6(c)): `Dillon v. Gloss, 256 U.S. 368, 376 (1921), quoted in Nixon v. United States, 506 U.S. 224, 230 (1993).`

If you can see the language only through the intermediate case and can't see the original's page, cite the intermediate case alone. Never supply the original's pincite from memory.
